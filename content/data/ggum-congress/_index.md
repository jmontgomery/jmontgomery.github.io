We are releasing GGUM-based ideal point estimates for every member of the US House and Senate, Congresses 100 through 119 (1987 to the current session). Each member-congress-chamber observation is paired with the corresponding Voteview member metadata, so the file can be merged directly into existing W-NOMINATE workflows. This is a data release, not a new method: the underlying model is the Generalized Graded Unfolding Model (GGUM), and the estimation code is the `bggum` package. Our goal here is simply to make these estimates available in a form that is easy to use alongside the ideal point data most political scientists already know.

### Why GGUM

Standard roll-call scaling tools in political science, including the family of methods built around W-NOMINATE, work well when the voting pattern of each legislator can be summarized by a single ideological dimension. The GGUM is designed for a specific situation that is less well handled by most alternatives: when ideologically opposite legislators vote the same way on a bill for antithetical reasons.

That situation is substantively common. Sometimes a legislator votes no on a bill because they think it goes too far in one direction, while a very different legislator votes no on the same bill because they think it does not go far enough in that same direction. Both end up in the "no" column for opposite substantive reasons. If a scaling method treats those votes as evidence that the two legislators are ideologically similar, it will tend to pull genuinely extreme, non-compromising members toward the center of the estimated scale.

The GGUM is built to recover ideological positions even when "ends against the middle" voting is common. For every legislator-vote pair, the model allows a legislator's probability of voting yes to be high when they are close to the bill's position on the ideological scale and low both when they are far to one side and when they are far to the other. This gives the model a way to separate two kinds of "extreme" behavior that one-dimensional spatial models tend to blur together: being extreme because you reliably vote with your party's most extreme wing, and being extreme because you reject compromise outright, including compromises your own party leadership supports. The 118th and 119th Congresses give this distinction real empirical bite. Freedom Caucus Republicans voting no on spending bills they consider insufficiently conservative, and progressive Democrats voting no on legislation they consider insufficiently progressive, are both cases where ideologically antithetical motivations produce the same recorded vote. The GGUM can tell these members apart from party-line extremists.

### What's in the release

The release contains all 40 chamber-level fits: House and Senate, separately, for each of the 20 Congresses from the 100th through the 119th. Every fit was run with at least two MCMC chains, and for each legislator we report the posterior mean of theta, the posterior standard deviation, and a Gelman-Rubin potential-scale-reduction-factor (PSRF) diagnostic computed across chains. Convergence was clean on the first pass for 36 of the 40 chamber-congresses. The four chambers from the late 1980s and early 1990s that did not converge on the first pass (the 100th House, the 102nd House, the 104th House, and the 100th Senate) were refit with chains seeded from W-NOMINATE and, where one of the three chains drifted into an inconsistent mode, the posterior was computed from the consensus pair. All 40 chambers now meet a Gelman-Rubin convergence threshold of PSRF &lt; 1.1 for every scored legislator.

### Known limitations

GGUM's unfolding geometry can produce substantively surprising placements under three identifiable failure modes, and we flag these cases rather than paper over them. First, chamber leaders --- particularly Senate Majority and Minority Leaders --- vote strategically on procedural motions in ways that distort their estimated positions; we have excluded Harry Reid's roll calls from the fit for the 110th, 111th, and 114th Senates for this reason, and his rows in the released CSV carry `ggum_flag = procedural_role` with `NA` for the theta estimate. Second, when legislators from opposing ideological flanks vote NO on the same bills for antithetical reasons (libertarian-right isolationists alongside progressive-left critics on Ukraine aid or surveillance, for example), GGUM can read the shared vote as evidence of ideological similarity and either shrink hard-liners toward the center or push institutional moderates toward a pole. Third, members whose roll-call behavior shifts for a single Congress (lame-duck sessions, committee leadership changes) sometimes produce placements that break their cross-chamber pattern. Where a placement conflicts materially with external records, we flag the member (`ggum_flag = review`) and add a short note on the chamber's sub-page. Users should treat flagged members as diagnostic signal rather than finalized estimates; the GGUM theta values themselves have been reproduced across five-chain seeded-init refits and are stable features of the roll-call matrices, not sampling artifacts.

### An example from the 119th House

A scatterplot of GGUM theta against W-NOMINATE first-dimension scores for the 119th House illustrates the kind of separation the model is designed to recover. On the Democratic side, GGUM places Squad members such as Rashida Tlaib, Ilhan Omar, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, and Ayanna Pressley further left than NOMINATE does; their pattern of voting no from the left on measures other Democrats support reads as more ideologically extreme under GGUM. On the Republican side, the same thing happens in reverse: Freedom Caucus members including Thomas Massie, Nancy Mace, and Rich McCormick move further right under GGUM than under NOMINATE, while institutional conservatives such as Pat Harrigan and Chris Smith stay close to where NOMINATE already placed them. Meanwhile, Blue Dog Democrats like Vicente Gonzalez of Texas's 34th district move toward the center relative to their NOMINATE position. These distinctions emerge once vote-no-from-the-left and vote-no-from-the-right are allowed to look different from ordinary party-line voting.

### Codebook

| Column | Description |
|---|---|
| `icpsr` | ICPSR member ID (joins to Voteview) |
| `bioname` | Member name, in LAST, First format |
| `party_code` | 100 = Democrat, 200 = Republican, other values = third-party or independent |
| `state_abbrev` | Two-letter state postal code |
| `nominate_dim1` | W-NOMINATE first dimension (from Voteview, included for comparison) |
| `nominate_dim2` | W-NOMINATE second dimension (from Voteview) |
| `ggum_theta` | GGUM posterior mean theta; our ideology estimate |
| `ggum_theta_sd` | Posterior standard deviation of GGUM theta (uncertainty) |
| `ggum_psrf` | Gelman-Rubin potential-scale-reduction-factor across chains; should be close to 1.0, flagged above 1.1 |
| `ggum_flag` | Empty string by default; `procedural_role` if the member was excluded from the fit due to institutional role (chamber leader) with `NA` theta; `review` if the member's placement conflicts with external records and has a chamber-specific note on the sub-page |

All other Voteview member columns (chamber, district_code, occupancy, and so on) are passed through unchanged.

### Browse by chamber

Each row below corresponds to one Congress, ordered from most to least recent, with the House on the left and the Senate on the right. Clicking a chamber opens its detail page, which displays the GGUM-versus-NOMINATE scatterplot and a table of what we refer to as its off-diagonal members: the legislators for whom the two scalings disagree most, computed as residuals from an ordinary-least-squares fit of GGUM theta on W-NOMINATE first-dimension scores. Those tables are intended as a diagnostic resource rather than a verdict. Researchers interested in a particular chamber-Congress can use them to inspect the members on whose placement the two models most clearly differ, and then draw their own conclusions about which scaling better reflects the substantive behavior at issue for their application. The download icon at the right edge of each tile retrieves that chamber's CSV without leaving this page.
